Naseem Shah is 21 years old, bowls at genuine pace, and shapes the ball both ways. He is also, quietly, the most complicated selection question Pakistan cricket is sitting on right now.

## The Real Issue

This is not simply about whether Naseem plays the next Test match. It is about whether Pakistan's cricket administration has a coherent, long-term plan for a fast bowler who could anchor the national attack for the next decade, or whether short-term pressures will chip away at him the way they have chipped away at so many Pakistani quicks before.

The history is not comforting. Waqar Younis carried heavy workloads through injury. Mohammad Amir's body bent under the demands of non-stop cricket across formats. Hasan Ali has spent as much time in rehabilitation as he has in the playing XI over the past three years. Pakistan has a pattern of burning through its fast bowlers, and Naseem, despite his obvious gifts, is not automatically exempt from that fate.

What makes this particular moment critical is that Naseem is now a first-choice name across all three formats. When a bowler of his type becomes indispensable in Test cricket, white-ball cricket, and T20 internationals simultaneously, the cumulative load becomes genuinely dangerous. His shoulder surgery in 2023 should have been the loudest possible warning signal. Whether the PCB heard it clearly enough remains an open question.

## The Workload Mathematics

Test cricket is the format that extracts the most from a fast bowler's body. Five-day matches, long spells on abrasive pitches, back-to-back Test series without adequate recovery windows — this is where careers are made and bodies are broken. Naseem's red-ball workload needs its own calculation, entirely separate from the PSL calendar, the T20I schedule, and the white-ball tour commitments that pile up around it.

The PCB's selection committee has to ask a question that no selection committee ever enjoys asking: which format does Naseem matter most to Pakistan in? The honest answer is Test cricket. White-ball replacements are easier to find. A red-ball fast bowler who swings the new ball at 145 kph and reads conditions intelligently is not.

That reality should inform every scheduling decision. It should mean Naseem is rested from T20I series when a Test tour is approaching. It should mean his PSL involvement, particularly mid-tournament bowling loads, is monitored in direct conversation with the national team's medical staff. Whether those conversations are actually happening at the institutional level, or whether fitness decisions remain reactive rather than proactive, is something the PCB has never been fully transparent about.

## What It Means Going Forward

If Pakistan wants Naseem to still be a 90-over-a-Test-match bowler at 28, the groundwork has to be laid now. That means accepting short-term inconvenience. It means dropping him from a T20I series even when selectors feel the pressure to field the strongest possible XI. It means building the bowling attack's depth so that Naseem's absence from a format does not feel like a crisis.

Shaheen Shah Afridi's own injury history demonstrates what happens when that depth is absent. Pakistan leaned on Shaheen across all formats because there was no one else credible enough to share the burden. By the time his knee gave way, Pakistan had no real cover and Shaheen had no real runway to recover without feeling the pressure to rush back. Naseem is now entering the phase of his career where the same trap could spring shut on him.

The emergence of Khurram Shahzad and the continued development of Mohammad Wasim Jr provide some reason for optimism. But those options need to be trusted and tested in live international cricket, not kept as theoretical alternatives while Naseem bowls every available over.

## What to Watch Next

The next major indicator will be how the PCB handles Naseem's scheduling around Pakistan's upcoming Test commitments. If he is rotated out of a white-ball series before a red-ball tour, that is a signal the board is managing him intelligently. If he plays every available format right up to the first Test and then picks up a soft-tissue injury in the opening match, it will confirm that Pakistan cricket is still learning the same lesson it has failed to learn for thirty years.

Watch the squad announcements. Watch the fitness bulletins. Watch whether Naseem completes a full year of Test cricket without a breakdown. Those will be the real answers.

Right now, Pakistan has arguably its best fast bowling talent since Shoaib Akhtar. The question is whether the system around him is good enough to protect that talent. The PCB has the information, the medical staff, and the recent cautionary examples. What it needs is the institutional discipline to act on all three.
