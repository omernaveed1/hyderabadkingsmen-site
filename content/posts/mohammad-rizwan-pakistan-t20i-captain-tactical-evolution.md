Mohammad Rizwan inherited the Pakistan T20I captaincy at a complicated moment. Babar Azam's exit from the role in late 2023 carried the weight of public debate, selection politics, and a team searching for a post-World Cup identity. Two years on, Rizwan's tenure deserves a clear-eyed assessment, free from the noise that surrounded how he got the job.

## The Angle: From Reactive to Proactive

The most significant shift under Rizwan has been attitudinal. Pakistan T20I cricket under various captains had a habit of setting conservative totals, waiting for opposition mistakes, and relying on one or two senior players to win matches single-handedly. Rizwan has, credit where it is due, pushed back against that culture.

His own batting is the clearest example. Once a pure anchor, Rizwan recalibrated his T20I strike rate substantially across this period, accepting that a captain cannot ask aggressive intent from his batters while modelling caution himself. The data backs this up: his powerplay scoring rate improved, and he began taking on short balls rather than nudging around them. It is a small thing on paper, but it changed the dressing room conversation.

Bowling changes have been the bigger talking point. Rizwan moved away from the rigid use of senior pacers in fixed spells, experimenting more openly with leg-spin in the powerplay and deploying part-time options to create matchup problems. Pakistan's willingness to throw the ball to Shadab Khan in the first six overs, something previous captains treated as unthinkable against certain opposition, became a recognisable tactical signature. It has not always worked. But the willingness to try is itself a departure from tradition.

## What Has Not Changed

Honesty demands acknowledging the gaps. Pakistan's middle-order volatility, a problem that predates Rizwan's captaincy by several years, has not been resolved. The number four and five positions remain a revolving door, partly a selection issue rather than a purely tactical one, but captains set the tone for how young batters are managed and protected. That tone has been inconsistent.

The death-bowling unit has also remained exposed on flat tracks. Rizwan has rotated his options and shown faith in younger seamers in certain conditions, but Pakistan still leak runs at the back end of innings against quality opposition at a rate that costs them matches. No captain fixes this alone, but tactical bowling restrictions and field placements at the death have not always reflected the boldness Rizwan shows elsewhere.

## What It Means Going Forward

The 2026 T20 World Cup sits on the horizon, and Pakistan's qualification picture, their preparation schedule, and the composition of their squad will all be shaped by decisions made in the next twelve to eighteen months. Rizwan's captaincy stability provides something valuable: continuity. Pakistan have, historically, been prone to mid-cycle captaincy changes that cost the team cohesion. Whatever one thinks of the appointment, the continuity itself has allowed younger players like Saim Ayub to develop within a settled environment rather than adjusting to a new captain's demands every few months.

The bigger question is whether Rizwan can evolve his approach against top-tier opposition. Pakistan's performances against India, Australia, and England in high-pressure knockout cricket remain the ultimate benchmark. A captain can look excellent in bilateral series and still fall short when it matters most. The tactical innovations need stress-testing at the highest level.

There is also the Babar Azam equation. The former captain remains in contention for selection as a batter, and managing that dynamic, the presence of a previous captain of Babar's stature in the XI, requires social intelligence as much as tactical acumen. So far, Rizwan has handled it without visible friction. That is worth noting.

## What to Watch Next

Pakistan's upcoming T20I assignments, particularly any tours to subcontinental conditions and any series against the top-ranked sides in the format, will tell us whether the tactical progress is real or just flattering records against lower-ranked opposition. Watch specifically for how Rizwan uses the powerplay with the ball on spin-friendly tracks, how he manages his middle-order conversation when batters are under pressure, and whether Pakistan's death-bowling plans look more structured than they did eighteen months ago.

Two years in, the verdict is this: Rizwan has moved the dial. Not dramatically, and not without setbacks. But Pakistan's T20I cricket is more tactically curious than it was when he took charge. In a format that rewards curiosity, that is a foundation worth building on.
