The question surfaces every few months, usually after a tense ICC tournament encounter or a diplomatic thaw that never quite holds: when will Pakistan and India play bilateral cricket again? The honest answer, uncomfortable as it is, shapes everything from PCB's long-term revenue projections to the credibility of the Asia Cup as a regional product.

## The Real Story Is Money and Politics, in That Order

Pakistan and India last played a bilateral series on Pakistani soil in 2012-13. That is more than a decade of a frozen fixture list between the two most populous cricket nations on earth. The romantic version of events says both boards desperately want the series and only governments stand in the way. The realistic version is considerably messier.

The Board of Control for Cricket in India carries enormous financial leverage. India's broadcasting rights are the engine that funds world cricket, including the ICC's revenue-sharing model from which PCB draws a meaningful portion of its annual income. BCCI has no pressing commercial incentive to push New Delhi for clearance because Indian fans consume Pakistan cricket at ICC events anyway. The product sells itself on neutral ground. A bilateral series would require political cover that neither government has shown appetite to provide, and without that cover, BCCI has little reason to absorb the institutional risk.

For PCB, the calculation runs the other way. A home series against India would be transformative, potentially the largest single revenue event in the board's history. That desperation, however real, weakens PCB's negotiating hand. Pakistan needs the series more visibly than India does, and both sides know it.

## The ICC Window Is the Only Realistic Near-Term Format

Since 2008, all Pakistan-India cricket has been played at ICC events or the Asia Cricket Council's Asia Cup. That structure is unlikely to change before 2027 at the earliest, and even that estimate is optimistic.

The Champions Trophy 2025, hosted by Pakistan with India playing their matches at a neutral venue in Dubai, is the closest thing to a diplomatic test case the cricket calendar currently offers. If that hybrid arrangement proceeds without serious incident and generates the expected broadcast numbers, it may quietly reset expectations. Both boards would walk away with data proving that a managed, politically buffered format can work commercially. Whether that translates into the political will for a full bilateral series is a separate and much harder question.

The Asia Cup, which rotates hosting rights and has already been subjected to hybrid models precisely because of this bilateral freeze, will next land in 2026. Its structure, single host or split, will itself be a signal of where relations stand.

## What a Realistic Timeline Actually Looks Like

Anyone projecting a bilateral series before 2027 is guessing generously. A more grounded sequence would require, in rough order: a sustained improvement in diplomatic relations at the government level in both countries, an explicit BCCI request to the Indian government for a no-objection clearance, a reciprocal PCB security assurance credible enough for the Indian board to present to its own broadcasters and sponsors, and finally a Future Tours Programme slot that both boards agree to protect.

Each of those steps has collapsed at least once in the last fifteen years. The 2015 Ufa summit between Prime Ministers Modi and Sharif briefly raised hopes before the Pathankot attack ended the conversation. The pattern of raised hopes and abrupt reversals has conditioned both fan bases to scepticism, and rightly so.

If, hypothetically, diplomatic conditions improved significantly after the 2026 elections cycle in both countries, a series could be pencilled into the 2027-28 FTP window. That remains a conditional, not a forecast.

## What to Watch

Three markers are worth tracking over the next twelve months. First, how the Champions Trophy 2025 hybrid model is received politically and commercially, because a smooth tournament resets the baseline for future negotiations. Second, whether BCCI and PCB hold any formal bilateral talks at the June 2025 ICC Annual Conference, which sometimes serves as a quiet back-channel. Third, the composition of Pakistan's next government and its stated posture toward India, because PCB's room to manoeuvre is always, in the end, bounded by Islamabad.

The series will happen eventually. Cricket's commercial logic alone guarantees that. But eventually, in this context, may mean a decade or more away. Pakistan cricket fans deserve honest commentary on that timeline rather than seasonal optimism that evaporates with the next border incident.

The game is bigger than any bilateral fixture. But this particular fixture is bigger than almost anything else in the game. That tension is not going away.
