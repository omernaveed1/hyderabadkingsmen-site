Pakistan's Test cricket has a recurring nightmare at the top of the order, and the selectors keep waking up, shrugging, and going back to sleep. With a demanding 2026-27 cycle on the horizon, the time for half-measures is over.

## The Real Problem Behind the Revolving Door

Since the retirement of Mohammad Hafeez and Ahmed Shehzad's fade from international relevance, Pakistan have cycled through opening combinations at a pace that would embarrass a county side in a relegation scrap. Shan Masood, Imam-ul-Haq, Abdullah Shafique, Saim Ayub, Fakhar Zaman in cameos — the list reads like a selection committee brainstorming session that never quite reached a conclusion.

The root issue is not talent. Pakistan has batters. The issue is that the selection philosophy keeps blurring the line between formats. A batter earns a T20I reputation, gets a Test look, struggles with the discipline required to leave the ball outside off stump for 40 minutes, and is quietly cycled out before anyone admits the experiment failed. The nation's top order ends up bearing the cost of that identity confusion.

Specialist Test opening is a craft. It demands a particular mindset: the willingness to survive a new ball in difficult conditions, to bat time rather than manufacture runs, and to absorb pressure so that the middle order — Babar Azam, Saud Shakeel, Mohammad Rizwan — can function in a more settled environment. Trying to develop that craft while simultaneously managing a player's confidence across three formats is asking too much of both the player and the system.

## What the 2026-27 Cycle Demands

The schedule Pakistan face in this period includes tours and home series that will test their Test credentials seriously. Conditions in South Africa, England, and Australia punish technically deficient openers brutally. The seaming new ball in those environments does not care about a batter's PSL form or their white-ball strike rate. It asks one question: can you leave, defend, and survive long enough to let your team-mates win the match?

This is precisely why the selection committee needs to draw a sharp line. Identify two or three batters who are genuinely built for red-ball opening — technically sound, mentally patient, physically equipped for five-day cricket — and back them across this cycle regardless of a bad series or two. Consistency of selection breeds consistency of performance. Pakistan have rarely given their openers that chance.

Abdullah Shafique showed enough in patches to warrant real backing. Saim Ayub, when fit, has the technique and temperament to develop into a long-term option, but he needs to know Test cricket is his primary brief, not a staging post between T20 assignments. There may also be domestic talent in the Quaid-e-Azam Trophy that deserves a harder look, particularly among Sindh and Karachi's domestic circuit, which has quietly produced technically correct top-order players who have not yet been handed a proper audition.

## Stop Raiding the Middle Order

There is a lazy habit that must stop: converting middle-order batters into makeshift openers when the top order fails. It solves nothing. It unsettles the batter asked to do an unfamiliar job, and it creates a false ceiling for their actual development. Babar Azam opening in Tests is a conversation that surfaces every time Pakistan lose early wickets in the first session. It should not be. His value at number three or four, building on a platform, is too important to fritter away on crisis management.

The selectors must ring-fence specialist roles. Openers open. Middle-order batters bat in the middle order. It sounds simple because it is simple, yet Pakistan keep complicating it.

## What to Watch Next

The immediate indicator will be how Pakistan name their squads for upcoming red-ball assignments. If the selectors default again to white-ball form as a shortcut to Test selection, expect more of the same fragile starts and chasing totals from positions of unnecessary difficulty.

Watch, also, how the domestic structure responds. The PCB's red-ball competitions need to be the genuine pipeline they are supposed to be. If a batter accumulates significant runs in the Quaid-e-Azam Trophy as an opener across multiple seasons and is still ignored, the system is broken at the source, not just at the selection table.

Pakistan's Test batting has the match-winners in Babar, Saud, and a bowling attack that can dismiss sides. What it lacks is the platform those match-winners need. Fixing the top of the order, properly and patiently, is the single biggest lever available to Pakistan Test cricket right now. The 2026-27 cycle is not a dress rehearsal. It is the exam.
