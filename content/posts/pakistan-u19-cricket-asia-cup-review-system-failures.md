## Glimpses of Promise, Familiar Frustrations

Pakistan's Under-19 side produced enough at the recent Asia Cup to make you sit up, and enough to make you sigh. The talent is there. The scaffolding around it is not, and that gap continues to define — and diminish — Pakistan's junior cricket.

## The Real Story: A Pipeline With Too Many Leaks

Strip away the match-by-match results and the bigger question is one Pakistan cricket has been asking for a decade: why do so few U-19 standouts make it through to sustained senior careers? The Asia Cup is a useful mirror here. India, Bangladesh, and Afghanistan have all used junior continental tournaments as genuine launchpads. Pakistan tends to use them as auditions that lead nowhere in particular.

The problem is structural. Selection for the U-19 set-up remains opaque, with regional academies feeding players unevenly into the national pool. Boys from Karachi and Lahore have clearer pathways than those from Hyderabad, Quetta, or Peshawar, not because talent is geographically concentrated, but because scouting networks and coaching resources are. The PCB's High Performance Centre in Lahore is a genuinely good facility. But one centre cannot correct a national imbalance.

There is also the age-fraud ghost, which never fully leaves the room in South Asian junior cricket. Suspicion about registered ages, whether founded or not, distorts team compositions and muddies performance analysis. Until the PCB enforces biometric verification with absolute consistency, this cloud will remain.

## What the Asia Cup Showed About This Particular Group

Without over-reading individual performances, this U-19 squad showed some things worth recording. The pace bowling unit carried genuine menace in patches — raw pace, which Pakistan has never lacked at junior level, was supplemented on occasions by smarter plans and better length discipline than recent editions of this side have offered. That is progress, even if incremental.

The batting, however, exposed the same old fragility under pressure. When conditions were helpful, strokeplay flowed. When the opposition applied heat or the pitch played tricks, the top order folded with a kind of institutional predictability. Middle-order anchoring, the ability to bat 40 overs rather than 25, remains the single most persistent weakness in Pakistan junior batting. Coaches at domestic level still over-reward attractive strokemakers and under-develop the temperament to grind.

The wicketkeeping-batting slot, critical in the modern white-ball game, also looked unconvincing. At a time when the likes of Dhruv Jurel and Mushfiqur Rahim lookalikes are emerging from rival academies as complete keeper-batters, Pakistan's junior glovemen look more comfortable behind the stumps than in front of them.

## What Needs to Change

First, the transition policy. The PCB must formalise what happens to U-19 players after major tournaments. Right now, too many of them disappear back into domestic obscurity without any structured senior exposure. A mandatory provincial first-class allocation within twelve months of an Asia Cup or World Cup appearance would at least force the system to keep watching these boys.

Second, coaching continuity. The U-19 head coach position has changed hands with disorienting frequency. A player who spends two years in the junior system might work under three different coaches with three different philosophies. The development of batting temperament, in particular, requires years of consistent messaging. That is impossible under a revolving door.

Third, scouting reach. The Hyderabad region, to take one example, has historically produced significant domestic talent, yet its representation in U-19 national squads is persistently thin relative to its output in grade cricket. This is a resource and attention problem. Regional talent identification camps need to be regular, rigorous, and genuinely open, not tokenistic tick-boxes.

## What to Watch Next

The ICC U-19 World Cup cycle is the next meaningful checkpoint. How the PCB selects the squad, whether it rewards the Asia Cup performers or reshuffles based on the usual behind-the-scenes pressures, will tell you a great deal about whether lessons are being absorbed. Watch also for which of this Asia Cup's standout bowlers receive provincial red-ball contracts. That is where you find out if the system is serious.

Pakistan junior cricket has never lacked for raw material. What it lacks is an industrial process patient enough and consistent enough to turn ore into something durable. Until that changes, the Asia Cup will remain what it has been for too long: a display of potential, not a launching pad for careers.
