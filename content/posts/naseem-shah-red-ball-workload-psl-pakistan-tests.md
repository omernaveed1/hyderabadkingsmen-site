## The Pacer Pakistan Cannot Afford to Burn

Every time Naseem Shah steams in with the red ball and produces one of those searing away-swingers, Pakistan cricket feels genuinely hopeful. Every time he limps off a field clutching his shoulder, his side, or his knee, that hope curdles into dread. The PCB's handling of Naseem's red-ball workload is no longer a medical footnote. It is the central selection question of this Pakistan Test era.

## What This Story Is Really About

This is not simply a fitness story. It is a structural problem rooted in how Pakistan cricket operates across three formats simultaneously, with an increasingly crowded international calendar and a PSL season that now sits firmly in the middle of the year's most demanding stretch.

Naseem is 21. He is already Pakistan's most potent Test new-ball option since Wasim and Waqar were operating in tandem. That is not hyperbole. It is the quiet consensus among those who have watched him dismantle batting line-ups in Australia and England. But fast bowlers of his type, high-action, high-pace, heavily reliant on shoulder rotation, operate on a finite physical budget. The PCB, and the national selection committee, are spending that budget faster than it can be replenished.

The core tension is this: Naseem is too valuable to rest, and too fragile to play everything. Neither the PCB nor the franchises have yet solved that equation. When the PSL comes around, franchise pressure and commercial obligations pull in one direction. When a Test series abroad beckons, national duty pulls in another. Naseem sits at the intersection of both, with no clear, publicly communicated policy governing how many overs he bowls across a rolling twelve-month period.

Other major cricketing nations have navigated versions of this problem. England rested James Anderson from white-ball cricket almost entirely for the back half of his career. Australia protected Mitchell Starc during IPL windows by simply not permitting him to participate. Pakistan has historically been reactive rather than proactive, managing Naseem's injuries after they occur rather than engineering the schedule to prevent them.

## What It Means Going Forward

If the PCB does not formalise a workload cap, the risks are straightforward and serious. A serious shoulder or stress fracture injury at this stage of Naseem's career could cost Pakistan twelve to eighteen months of his services at the precise moment the Test team is rebuilding under Shan Masood's captaincy. Pakistan's next major red-ball assignments, series in England and home Tests against challenging opposition, require Naseem operating at full tilt.

A sensible model would involve the selectors declaring, early in each year, which formats Naseem will prioritise in which windows. If a long Test tour is scheduled, his PSL involvement should be scaled back, not eliminated, but managed, with a firm over-count ceiling agreed with any franchise he represents. That ceiling should be built around a medical target, not around what a franchise wants from its marquee signing or what a broadcaster wants for its viewing figures.

There is also a development argument. Pakistan needs a genuine second seamer to share the new-ball burden. Shaheen Shah Afridi is the partner in theory, but Shaheen has his own injury history. The pipeline beyond them, Mohammad Wasim Jr., Khurram Shahzad, and others, needs competitive red-ball overs. If the PCB protects Naseem intelligently, those bowlers get opportunities. If it does not, Pakistan may end up with an exhausted Naseem and an underdeveloped support cast simultaneously.

## What to Watch Next

The immediate marker is how the selectors treat Naseem during the next PSL season relative to the Test assignments surrounding it. If he is handed a full franchise workload and then immediately expected to lead the attack in a Test series, nothing will have changed and the cycle will repeat.

Watch also for whether the PCB publishes, or at least communicates publicly through briefings, any kind of structured workload policy for its fast-bowling assets. The absence of transparency has historically allowed short-term commercial thinking to override long-term cricketing sense.

And watch Naseem himself. He is young enough to be managed, ambitious enough to push through pain, and talented enough that the temptation to keep playing him will always override caution if no system exists to enforce rest. Pakistan cricket has wasted genuinely special bowlers before through exactly this kind of benign neglect. It cannot afford to do it again.
