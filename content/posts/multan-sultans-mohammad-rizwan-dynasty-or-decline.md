Mohammad Rizwan has made Multan Sultans the most successful PSL franchise of the modern era. Two titles, a culture of ruthless professionalism, and a captain who bats like a machine in white-ball cricket. Yet the question Pakistan cricket fans are increasingly asking is whether the Sultans have peaked, or whether this is simply the lull before another charge.

## The Rizwan Effect

It would be lazy to reduce Multan's success to one man, but it would be dishonest to pretend the architecture of this franchise does not begin and end with Rizwan. He does not just contribute runs. He sets the tempo of a chase, stabilises a collapse, and holds the dressing room to a standard of preparation that other PSL squads have visibly struggled to match.

When Rizwan is in form, Multan are a different proposition to every other team in the competition. His strike rate has drawn criticism, and fairly so in certain passages of play, but his conversion rate and his ability to be there at the death, with the equation still manageable, has been worth more to Sultans than any fireworks merchant at the top.

The franchise has also built sensibly around him. Shahid Afridi in the coaching setup brought tactical clarity. Intelligent overseas selections, particularly in the pace bowling department, have given Multan firepower beyond the batting.

## Where the Cracks Are Showing

Here is where the honest analysis gets uncomfortable for Sultans fans. The PSL landscape is shifting. Lahore Qalandars rebuilt, invested in youth pipelines, and came back harder. Peshawar Zalmi have depth that does not always show in standings. And now, with the Hyderabad Kingsmen entering the competition, the talent pool across the league is being stretched even thinner, which cuts both ways for established sides.

Multan's over-reliance on Rizwan is also a structural risk. He is now a permanent fixture in Pakistan's national setup across all three formats, which means fatigue is a real factor. A Rizwan who is 60 percent physically is still a fine cricketer, but he is no longer the difference-maker the Sultans need him to be.

The middle order, at various points, has looked brittle once Rizwan falls. When the captain departs early, the innings has occasionally disintegrated with a speed that exposes how thin the leadership within the batting unit actually runs. That is a recruitment problem as much as a form problem, and it is one the franchise management must address rather than paper over with another international marquee signing.

## What Comes Next

The Sultans' path forward depends almost entirely on two decisions. First, whether they can develop a genuine number-three or number-four batter who provides stability independent of Rizwan's presence at the crease. The Pakistan domestic circuit has options. Scouting them requires intent, not just budget.

Second, and more critically, the franchise needs to start thinking about a post-Rizwan captaincy plan. That is not a morbid thought. It is basic franchise management. Rizwan is in his early thirties, at the peak of his powers right now, but the PSL franchises that sustain success across half a decade are those that build institutional identity rather than personality cults.

Lahore's ability to survive and thrive despite losing key figures at various junctures is instructive. The Sultans should be studying that model closely.

## What to Watch

Keep an eye on how Multan approach the next PSL draft and retention window. If they double down on Rizwan and load the squad with more international names around him, that signals a short-term mentality, another tilt at the title on the back of one man's brilliance. If instead they invest in younger Pakistani talent and build a more collective identity, it would suggest the franchise is thinking beyond the next season.

Also watch how Rizwan is managed nationally. The PCB's increasing workload demands on their premier white-ball players will eventually create tension with franchise schedules. Multan, more than any other PSL side, feel that tension most acutely.

Dynasty or decline? Right now, it is neither. It is a franchise at a crossroads, successful enough to believe it does not need to change, but vulnerable enough that continuing unchanged carries genuine risk. The choices made in the next twelve months will define whether the Rizwan era is remembered as the foundation of something lasting, or the high point of something already past.
