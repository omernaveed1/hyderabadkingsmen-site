Pakistan cricket has rarely possessed a fast bowler as naturally gifted as Naseem Shah at this stage of his career. Managing him correctly could define a generation of Test cricket. Getting it wrong could break him entirely.

## The Real Question Behind the Rotation Debate

On the surface, this looks like a standard workload discussion, the kind every major cricket board navigates with their premium quicks. But Naseem's case is different, and the PCB would be naive to treat it as routine.

Naseem turns 22 this year. He has already endured shoulder surgery, a procedure that shelved him for the better part of a year and stripped Pakistan's pace attack of its most dangerous component at the worst possible time. He returned looking sharp, his skiddy length and sharp bouncer as threatening as ever, but pace bowlers who have gone under the knife once are statistically far more vulnerable to re-injury when exposed to heavy red-ball loads across formats and tours without structured rest.

The PCB's selection panel, and head coach Jason Gillespie before his departure, spoke frequently about protecting Pakistan's fast bowling assets. The rhetoric has been correct. The execution has been patchier.

## What This Story Is Really About

This is not really about whether Naseem plays one Test more or less in a given series. It is about whether Pakistan cricket has a serious, science-backed workload framework, or whether selection decisions continue to be reactive, driven by immediate match demands rather than long-term athlete management.

The contrast with England is instructive, even if uncomfortable. The ECB and their medical staff track bowling loads obsessively, using over counts, pace data and recovery metrics to decide when a bowler sits out a red-ball game. Pakistan have the resources to do something similar. The question is whether the institutional will exists.

Naseem is also a T20I asset Pakistan simply cannot ignore. He bowls with genuine pace in the powerplay and at the death, a rare combination. The temptation to keep picking him across all three formats is understandable. It is also exactly the kind of thinking that shortens careers.

If the PCB wants Naseem bowling at full tilt in Test cricket at 28, they need to make hard choices now, at 22. That might mean resting him from a home Test series when the pitch conditions do not demand his particular skills. It might mean leaving him out of a T20I tri-series that Pakistan do not need to win at all costs. These are unpopular decisions. They are also the correct ones.

## What This Means Going Forward

The upcoming Test commitments, including tours where Pakistan have historically relied on pace to stay competitive, will test the PCB's resolve. Pakistan's bowling depth has improved with the emergence of Khurram Shahzad and the occasional promise shown by Abbas, but neither is a like-for-like replacement for Naseem's pace and aggression.

That gap tempts selectors to keep Naseem in the XI regardless of workload signals. Resisting that temptation requires trusting the process and accepting the short-term risk of a weaker attack in a single match over the long-term risk of another serious injury.

The other dimension is Naseem's own mentality. Fast bowlers are famously poor at admitting fatigue, particularly proud young Pakistanis who know their place in the eleven is only as secure as their last performance. The PCB and team management need to create an environment where Naseem feels safe raising concerns without fearing for his spot. That cultural shift matters as much as any selection formula.

## What to Watch Next

Three things will tell us whether the PCB is serious about this.

First, watch whether Naseem is rested for any upcoming limited-overs fixtures ahead of a red-ball tour, even when Pakistan's opponents are strong and public pressure demands a full-strength squad.

Second, watch how the team management communicates his absences. Vague references to 'workload management' with no detail breed suspicion and rumour. Transparency builds trust.

Third, and most tellingly, watch over counts. If Naseem is consistently bowling 20-plus overs in a Test match and then turning up for T20Is four days later, the workload framework is failing regardless of what the official line says.

Pakistan cricket's recent history is littered with fast bowlers who promised everything and delivered heartbreak, through injury, through burnout, through mismanagement. Naseem Shah looks like the real thing. Treating him that way means protecting him, even when it hurts in the short run.
